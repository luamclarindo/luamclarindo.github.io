Hypercuíca is an augmented cuíca, an Afro-Brazilian friction drum, developed through an ongoing process of experimentation with bodily gesture, acoustic sound, digital processing and human–machine musical interaction.

A clip-on microphone captures the cuíca's acoustic sound. The signal is processed in Ableton Live and returned through loudspeakers, while the instrument's acoustic voice remains audible throughout. Across different configurations, controllers, a smartphone's sensors and ready-made software devices have been used to explore how physical movement can shape sound and, more recently, invite a machine co-improviser into the performance.

This page documents the ongoing development of Hypercuíca through the systems and configurations explored so far, from early MIDI control to motion-based interaction and machine co-improvisation. The systems are presented chronologically; they are not a sequence of replacements, but different configurations that investigate distinct relationships between gesture and sound. New mappings and configurations will be added as the research progresses.

Supplementary material for the NIME paper

Video demonstration accompanying the NIME paper.

The paper combines technical documentation of the instrument and its gesture mappings with historical and cultural perspectives on the cuíca. It discusses earlier experiments with the instrument by Brazilian percussionists, including Galante's work with the puita, Naná Vasconcelos and Paulinho Bicolor. The article therefore offers more than a technical account: it also traces a broader musical context for imagining new possibilities with the cuíca.

Before the PhD: computer music and cuíca experiments

My experiments with the cuíca began well before the PhD, as did my interest in making music with computers. Some of my earliest encounters with computer music were playful and exploratory: Mario Paint, Windows Sound Recorder (Gravador de som), and composing experimental pieces with Noteworthy Composer. These experiences gradually developed into processing acoustic sounds and improvising with Ableton Live. Working with acoustic instruments and live electronics was already part of my practice before the doctoral project.

Gesture vocabulary

From System 2 onwards, a smartphone attached to the cuíca provides motion data. Two axes are used: up/down tilt and left/right rotation. Continuous mappings respond across a range of movement; toggles are triggered when a gesture reaches a threshold or an extreme. The exact functions and thresholds vary between configurations.

Systems designed so far

System 0 · Before the PhD

MIDI controller, footswitch and Ableton Live

A direct predecessor to the doctoral research · 2024

This early configuration amplified the cuíca into Ableton Live, where sound was shaped by hand and foot. Knobs on a MIDI controller provided continuous control, while a footswitch triggered a toggle. One press froze the delay and handed control of its left and right delay times to an LFO. The two times moved in opposite directions, so the frozen echo continued to drift rather than remaining static.

FootswitchToggle: freeze the delay and activate the LFO behaviour.
MIDI controllerContinuous knobs and MIDI notes for effect parameters.

In the mapping print, the LFO remote ranges move the left delay time from 88% to 22% and the right delay time from 19% to 81%.

8 June 2024 · Espacio Lahipoteca, Madrid · Organised by Raras Musicas Madrid.

System 1 · With Test Death, Bragança

Novation controller, footswitch and phone light

Novation + footswitch + phone sensors → plugdata inside Ableton Live

Named Test Death because this was the configuration used in a performance opening a concert by the Brazilian band Test Death in Bragança. It combined two parallel control layers. A Novation Launchkey MK3 controller and footswitch acted directly on Ableton Live's MIDI mappings. A phone's light sensor, read through ZIG SIM and OSC, controlled the frequency of an oscillator inside a plugdata patch. The oscillator was multiplied with the cuíca input, producing amplitude/ring modulation and a metallic, sideband-rich timbre. The patch also contained routing for phone gravity data to a drive stage and a threshold-triggered table looper. This was before the use of Max for Live in the project.

Control sourceFunction
Novation / footswitchDirect Live control of delay wet and feedback, freeze, LFO, Shifter, ring modulation, looper repeat and return sends.
Phone lightScaled to oscillator frequency (approximately 100–500 Hz), modulating the cuíca input.
Phone gravityPatch routing for a tanh drive stage and threshold-triggered table recording/playback.

The amplitude-modulation patch produced one of the timbres that remained especially meaningful to the subsequent development of the instrument.

System 2 · Doctoral project defence · University of Aveiro, 2025

The defence patch

ZIG SIM gravity → Max for Live

Developed for the doctoral project defence at the University of Aveiro in 2025, this was the first configuration to bring the phone's gravity sensor and the main control logic into a single Max for Live patch. The Shifter remained active, while gesture continuously shaped drive, envelope following, reverb and delay. Upward tilt increased a drive/reduction macro combining Redux, Drum Buss, saturation, compression and fine pitch detuning. Rotation controlled the Shifter's envelope-follow amount across the sweep, with a reverb wash growing to the left and delay/feedback growing to the right. The only toggle was the Hybrid Reverb freeze.

↑ Tilt upContinuous drive and reduction: Redux, Drum Buss, saturation, glue makeup and fine pitch.
↺ Rotate leftIncreases the Hybrid Reverb wash.
↻ Rotate rightIncreases delay and feedback.
Whole rotation sweepControls the Shifter envelope follower (−24 to +24 semitones); Shifter stays on.
FreezeThe only toggle: Hybrid Reverb freeze.

Feedback from earlier performances informed an attempt to make the relationship between bodily movement and sound modulation more perceptible, while reducing the computer's visual prominence on stage.

System 3 · NIME 2026

The NIME mapping

Gravity → Max for Live · three module toggles + two continuous macros

Presented in “Hypercuíca: Augmenting and Performing with an Afro-Brazilian Friction Drum” (NIME 2026, London), this mapping combines structural switches with continuous control. A full left rotation toggles the Shifter, a full right rotation toggles the Resonators, and a full downward tilt toggles the Delay. Between these extremes, the gestures continuously shape the active transformations.

↺ Full leftToggle: Shifter on/off.
↻ Full rightToggle: Resonators on/off.
↓ Full tilt downToggle: Delay on/off.
↑ Tilt upContinuous macro across delay, feedback, reverb, resonator decay, Shifter, EQ, saturation and gain.
Rotation sweepContinuous macro across panning, Shifter pitch, delay time and LFO amount.

The mappings were refined iteratively for ergonomic use, gestural clarity and expressive continuity. They describe a functional configuration rather than a prescriptive or definitive mapping.

System 4 · Frictionary · Intermediartes 2026

A vocabulary of gesture-based sonic modulations

Natural ↔ Radical · continuous gestures + stochastic reconfiguration

Frictionary joins “friction” (how the cuíca makes sound) and “dictionary” (a named vocabulary of gestures). Inspired by the idea of an actual interactive system, this configuration was developed for a paper presented at Intermediartes 2026. It was used to record an album, rather than performed publicly. The system introduces two processing modes and two interaction types.

GestureActionModeResult
Tilt downDrive itNatural · continuousIncreases saturation.
Rotate leftCave itNatural · continuousIncreases reverb.
Rotate rightEcho itNatural · continuousControls delay.
Tilt up (~75°)Flip itGlobal · toggleSwitches between Natural and Radical.
Tilt down (~75°)Topsy-turvyGlobal · triggerRe-rolls the effect chain, parameter start values and gesture assignments.
Tilt or rotateGhost itRadical · continuousModulates randomly assigned parameters.

Natural mode extends the instrument's qualities through continuous saturation, reverb and delay. Radical mode introduces stochastic processes. Each downward Topsy-turvy gesture re-rolls three layers: which effects are active (up to four of Grain Delay, Shifter, Resonator and Pitch), their initial parameter values, and which parameters are assigned to tilt or rotation. The gesture therefore does not only control the sound; it renegotiates its own control structure. Tilting up returns to Natural mode.

System 5 · Flowfal + Somax2

Motion control and a machine co-improviser

Ready-made plugins · no custom Max for Live programming

This configuration replaces ZIG SIM with FlowfalMap devices and introduces Somax2 as a machine co-improviser. Two FlowfalMap instances read the phone's Roll (rotation) and Pitch (tilt). The cuíca feeds two Ableton Live tracks, an Influencer and a Player, which drive Somax2. Each track has its own convolution reverb: an Arena hall of about 20 seconds on the Influencer and larger spaces on the Player.

↻ Rotate rightRaises reverb decay and wet level on both tracks, while moving their panning controls in opposite directions.
↑ Tilt upRaises Somax2's probability parameter, so the machine responds more often.

The corpus contains 20 minutes of cuíca improvisation recorded with the same microphone, favouring slow, whale-like sonorities over repetitive material. The spacious source material shapes the character of the machine's responses. A current limitation is that Somax2 for Live builds its corpus offline; using Somax2 for Max to build the corpus while playing is a next step to explore.

Creative explorations

Alongside the development of the numbered systems, the cuíca has been a point of departure for other musical, performative and audiovisual experiments. These works do not all use motion sensors: some combine the instrument with MIDI control and sound-processing plugins, while others bring pre-recorded voice, dance or visual manipulation into the performance.

Configurations in dialogue

In a more recent performance setup, two Ableton Live tracks run in parallel: one uses the NIME mapping (system 3), and the other uses the earlier doctoral project defence mapping (system 2). The module toggles of system 3 and the always-on Shifter, grit, envelope, reverb and delay behaviour of system 2 act on the cuíca together. This configuration treats the mappings as complementary resources rather than mutually exclusive versions.

Ongoing development

Hypercuíca remains an open research and performance project. The configurations documented here are snapshots of an evolving instrument: mappings may be refined, combined, or reconsidered, and new sensing strategies, processing systems and forms of human–machine interaction may emerge. This page will grow alongside that process.

This page is an evolving account based on the working mapping catalogue (November 2026). Some historical implementation details and exact control assignments remain subject to verification and may be refined in future updates.
